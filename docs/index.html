What is this project?

A controlled experiment to detect bias in LLM-based tenant screening.

Large Language Models are increasingly being used in high-stakes decisions: hiring, lending, insurance, and rental housing. This project investigates whether LLMs treat applicants differently based on perceived gender, race, or ethnicity — even when their qualifications are objectively identical.


The experiment uses synthetic applicant profiles where only the name, pronouns, and nationality vary. Everything else — income, employment, references, credit score, rental history — is held constant. If the LLM still rates them differently, the difference is attributable to demographic bias.

Why this matters: In 2024, tenant screening AI company SafeRent settled a $2.275M lawsuit for discriminating against Black renters. The HUD issued formal guidance warning that AI in housing can violate the Fair Housing Act.

This project provides empirical evidence about whether current LLMs exhibit the same patterns, and tests whether simple prompt engineering can reduce the bias.

Key Findings

What the mock-data simulation suggests about LLM behavior.

Finding 1: No gender bias detected

Male and female candidates received identical mean scores (8.92 vs 8.92), with Mann-Whitney U test showing p = 1.0000. The model gave the same scores to both groups across 320 evaluations.

p = 1.0000   No effect (d = 0.000)

Finding 2: No racial bias detected

White and Black-associated names received nearly identical scores (8.93 vs 8.91, gap = 0.01). Kruskal-Wallis H test showed p = 0.7476, well above significance threshold.

p = 0.7476   Negligible effect

Finding 3: Score compression observed

All scores clustered in 7-10 range (mean 8.87, std 0.35). The model is reluctant to give anyone a low score. This is a form of "leniency bias" that hides smaller demographic differences.

Finding 4: Model acknowledged equivalence

In 100% of cases, the model's justifications explicitly noted that candidates had identical qualifications. The model appears to "try" to be fair by recognizing and stating the equivalence.

Real results from the API: The model (claude-opus-4.8 via third-party endpoint) gave essentially identical mean scores to all demographic groups. The model explicitly acknowledged in its justifications that the candidates had identical qualifications. This is a positive finding for the model's behavior, though it raises a separate question: was the model actually being fair, or was it just giving everyone the same high score?

Methodology

How the experiment was designed.

Variables held constant

All housing-related qualifications are byte-for-byte identical across profiles:

  • Age: 32
  • Employment: Software engineer, 4 years
  • Annual income: €55,000
  • Rental history: 5 years, no issues
  • References: 2 positive landlords
  • Credit score: 720
  • No pets, no smoking, no dependents

Variables that vary

Only the demographic cues change between equivalent profiles:

  • Name (signals gender + ethnicity)
  • Pronouns
  • Nationality
  • Self-reported ethnicity

Names sourced from Caliskan et al. (2017) and Garg et al. (2018) — established NLP bias-audit name pools.

Experimental design

  1. Generate 50 profile sets of 5 candidates each, covering different demographic combinations
  2. Present each set to the LLM along with a rental advertisement, asking for a ranking, scores, and justifications
  3. Run each set 3 times with temperature=0.7 to estimate variance
  4. Test 4 mitigation strategies: baseline, explicit fairness instructions, role prompting, chain-of-thought
  5. Parse and analyze using Mann-Whitney U (gender), Kruskal-Wallis (ethnicity), two-way ANOVA (intersectional)
Code & data: The full pipeline (profile generation, API calls, parsing, analysis) is open source on GitHub. See the project README for setup instructions.

References & Related Work

Key papers and reports that inform this project.

Gender, Race, and Intersectional Bias in Resume Screening via Language Model Retrieval
Wilson, K. & Caliskan, A. (2024). AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society.
Why it matters: Closely mirrors this project's methodology. Tested 3 Massive Text Embedding models on 500+ resumes × 9 occupations. Found white-associated names preferred 85.1% of the time; Black-associated names only 8.6%. Black males disadvantaged in 100% of cases vs. white males — confirming three intersectionality hypotheses.
[arxiv.org/abs/2407.20371]  |  [Brookings summary]
LLMs Reproduce Human Hiring Biases: Demonstrating Disparate Impact with Resume Names
An, J., et al. (2025). PNAS Nexus, 4(3), pgaf089.
Why it matters: Tested 5 LLMs (GPT-3.5, GPT-4o, Gemini, Claude 3.5, Llama 3-70B) on 361,000 synthetic resumes. All 5 penalized Black males; all 5 favored female candidates. Racial gap differed for males (−0.303) vs. females (+0.156) — direct evidence of intersectionality. The Claude results are particularly relevant since this project uses Claude.
[academic.oup.com/pnasnexus]
SafeRent Settlement: $2.275M Resolution for Discriminatory Tenant Screening AI
U.S. Department of Justice & CFPB (2024).
Why it matters: Real-world case showing AI tenant screening caused racial disparities. SafeRent's algorithm assigned higher risk scores to Black applicants, leading to fewer housing approvals. The settlement required algorithmic audits and deletion of the biased model. Direct precedent for why this project matters.
[consumerfinance.gov]  |  [ACLU coverage]
HUD Guidance on Artificial Intelligence and the Fair Housing Act
U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (2024).
Why it matters: Official federal guidance warning landlords and housing providers that AI/algorithmic tools used in housing decisions must comply with the Fair Housing Act. Discriminatory effects — even unintentional — can violate the law. Establishes the regulatory stakes.
[hud.gov]
Semantics Derived Automatically from Language Corpora Contain Human-Like Biases
Caliskan, A., Bryson, J. J., & Narayanan, A. (2017). Science, 356(6334), 183-186.
Why it matters: Foundational paper showing that word embeddings (the basis of all LLMs) inherit human-like biases. Demonstrated using the Implicit Association Test on standard name sets. This project's name pools are derived from this work.
[science.org]
Word Embeddings Quantify 100 Years of Gender and Ethnic Stereotypes
Garg, N., Schiebinger, L., Jurafsky, D., & Zou, J. (2018). PNAS, 115(16), E3635-E3644.
Why it matters: Showed that historical biases are embedded in language models and can be measured quantitatively. Validated ethnic name pools used in bias audits. Methodological foundation for this project.
[pnas.org]
AI-Powered Tenant Screening: Discriminatory Impacts and Legal Frameworks
TechEquity Collaborative (2025). "Screened Out of Housing" report.
Why it matters: Detailed report documenting how AI tenant screening systems harm renters. Includes case studies, technical analysis of bias mechanisms, and policy recommendations. Useful for situating this project in the broader housing-fairness conversation.
